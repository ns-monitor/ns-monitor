SD1 / SD2 Ratio: -

SD1: Short-term Variability (Reading-to-reading spikes)
  • Excellent (< 1.0 mmol/L): Highly stable, smooth trend lines with minimal erratic jumps.
  • Moderate (1.0 to 1.6 mmol/L): Average variability expected in standard Type 1 diabetes management.
  • High (> 1.6 mmol/L): Erratic control with rapid, unpredictable spikes and crashes.
SD2: Long-term Variability (Overarching daily swings)
  • Excellent (< 3.0 mmol/L): Tightly bound long-term ranges without prolonged excursions.
  • Moderate (3.0 to 4.0 mmol/L): Standard variability, reflecting expected post-meal rises or gentle swings.
  • High (> 4.0 mmol/L): Sub-optimal control with massive, sweeping highs and lows throughout the day.
SD1 / SD2 Ratio (Predictability)
  • Excellent (< 0.2): Smooth, predictable curves. The overall trend (SD2) happens gently without jagged, short-term noise (SD1).
  • High / Sub-optimal (Higher values): A jagged, chaotic glucose profile. Rapid, short-term spikes dominate the overall curve, making dosing difficult.

J-Index: The Glycaemic Distress Score

This Period: -

Comparison Period: -

-

The J-Index is a "weighted" metabolic stress metric designed to solve the primary flaw of standard averages: the fact that a high glucose level is significantly more dangerous when it is also volatile. In standard clinical reports, a Mean Glucose and a Standard Deviation (SD) are treated as two separate, linear values. The J-Index acknowledges their interaction by squaring their sum:
J = 0.324 × (Mean + SD)²
(Note: The 0.324 constant is the unit-standardised multiplier for mmol/L data.)
By squaring the values, the J-Index creates a Risk Gradient. If your average glucose rises slightly, the score increases. If your variability increases slightly, the score increases. But if both rise together, the score skyrockets. This makes it an incredibly sensitive "early-warning system" for systemic failure. It represents the total "friction" your body and your loop are experiencing.

Troubleshooting a Rising J-Index

Step 1: The "Why" (Mean vs. SD)
Is the J-Index rising because your Mean moved or because your SD moved?

  • If Mean is up, SD is stable: You have a "baseline shift" (illness, site failure, activity change).
  • If SD is up, Mean is stable: You have "increased chaos." Look at the kinematics.

Step 2: The "When" (Look at CONGA)
Pull up the 24-Hour Reproducibility Profile. Look for the "Chaos Windows"—the peaks on the graph. If a rise correlates with a specific peak, adjust that specific window (e.g., Breakfast ICR).

Step 3: The "How" (Look at Rate of Change/Kurtosis)
Low/Falling Kurtosis: If the "mountain" is flattening, your loop is "hunting" or over-reacting. Check AAPS "Max SMB" or "Max Basal" limits.

Step 4: The Loop Efficiency Audit
If J-Index and Manual Bolus % are both rising, you are likely "rage-bolusing." This manual interference increases volatility and penalises the score.

Summary Logic:
J-Index tells you that there is a problem.
CONGA tells you when the problem is happening.
ROC Kurtosis tells you how the loop is reacting to it.

24-Hour Reproducibility Profile

CONGA-n measures how consistently your glucose returns to a similar level n hours later, at each time of day. For example, CONGA-2 compares every 11:00 reading across the period to what your glucose was at the corresponding 09:00 two hours earlier — a peak at 11:00 in the chart means those 2-hour swings are typically bigger at that time than at others. That chart is a 24-hour modal-day profile, built from many time-of-day buckets; the Single numerical result you get per timeframe pools every reading in the whole period into one figure instead, with no time-of-day structure, so it won't always match the chart's peaks exactly.

What each timeframe measures:

  • CONGA-1 (1h): short-term, moment-to-moment swings — a high value usually points to fast carbs or a rapid crash (insulin stacking, exercise).
  • CONGA-2 (2h): roughly matches a typical post-meal cycle — the size of the swing from a pre-meal baseline up to, and back down from, its peak.
  • CONGA-4 (4h): longer recovery and basal stability — whether glucose settles back to baseline within a normal insulin action window, or drifts because basal rates don't quite match.

CONGA needs at least 2 days selected to calculate anything — with only 1 day, each time-of-day slot has just one reading, with nothing yet to compare it to.

Rate of Change/Kurtosis

-
ROC Velocity Smoothness: This histogram tracks the "vibration" of your glucose. Each bar represents how often your glucose was moving at a specific speed. A tall, narrow peak at the 0.00 mark is the gold standard; it means your loop is successfully holding you at a standstill. If the bars are spreading out to the left and right, your glucose is in constant motion, indicating "sawtooth" turbulence and a less stable system.
