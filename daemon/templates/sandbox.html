Linear Detrending Toggle

What this control does:

Calculates the multi-year straight-line trajectory of your data and subtracts that gradual upward or downward drift before analyzing repeating rhythms.

Why it matters:
  • Removes Multi-Year Drift: Over several years, overall insulin demand or glucose variability may steadily decline as management improves. This long-term drift acts like a gentle ramp across the dataset.
  • Prevents False Low-Frequency Cycles: Frequency algorithms can mistake a multi-year downward ramp for a massive, slow-moving wave. Detrending flattens this multi-year baseline so the tool measures true repeating cycles rather than long-term progress.
  • Sharpens Phase Curves: On the Phase-Folded Wave Inspector (Tier 3), detrending prevents older years (when numbers were higher) from artificially smearing the shaded variation band against newer years.
When to keep it turned ON (Recommended):

Keep detrending enabled when scanning for hardware cycles, weekly routines, or annual seasons across multiple years of records.

When to turn it OFF:

Turn it off only if you want to inspect absolute historical values without adjusting for long-term clinical baseline changes.

Tier 3 — Phase-Folded Wave Inspector

What this graph does:

This tool slices your entire multi-year history into recurring blocks of your chosen cycle length and stacks them on top of each other. This reveals what happens on Day 1 versus Day 2, Day 3, and so on.

How to read it:
  • Solid Line (Median): The typical trend across the cycle. A flat line means the cycle has no real effect; a clear curve (like a dip or rise) indicates a genuine repeating pattern.
  • Shaded Band (IQR): The spread of normal daily variation. A narrow band indicates high consistency across cycles.
  • Phase Coherence (%): Measures how neatly cycles align. Scores above 80% indicate strong resonance; lower scores suggest background noise or an echo from a different cycle.
  • Phase Spread (Δ): The real-world difference between the highest and lowest points of the cycle (e.g., a 3% swing in variability).

Tier 2B — Multi-Year Annual Calendar Heatmap

What this graph does:

This raster maps every single day of your data on an unsmoothed calendar grid. Each row represents a calendar year (January on the left, December on the right), with day-to-day values color-coded from low (green) to high (orange/red).

How to read it:
  • Vertical Alignment Equals Real Cycles: Because whole years are stacked directly on top of each other, genuine seasonal patterns form vertical bands across consecutive rows.
  • Spotting Seasonal Shifts: If the mid-year columns (June, July, August) glow warm across multiple years while early months stay cool, you are looking at visual proof of winter insulin resistance.
  • Spotting Baseline Improvements: If the left side of older years is orange but newer years are solid green, your baseline stability has improved over time.

How to interpret these empirical results: ns-monitor Statistical Verification
  • No Pre-assigned Labels: The algorithm scans the full continuum from 2 to 400 days and identifies only peaks that statistically rise above the 99% False Alarm Probability ceiling.
  • Phase-Folding as the Ultimate Truth Test: If a peak is a genuine physical rhythm (such as a 14-day sensor change or a 3.5-day pod replacement), folding all observations creates a stable, repeatable waveform with a low p-value. An artifact or random noise collapses into a flat horizontal line (p > 0.10).
  • Why Mid-Frequency is Clean: In raw daily data, the 28–35 day zone sits at the noise floor. This empirically verifies that any monthly sinusoidal wave seen on rolling graphs was induced by the moving-average filter (the Slutsky-Yule theorem).
